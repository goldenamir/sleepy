# Business development plan

## 1. Purpose and decision to reach

Build a credible case for a business that solves a recurring problem the founder experiences with CPAP and sleep data. The first investment decision is whether one identifiable customer group has a problem worth paying to solve, and whether Sleepy can deliver the outcome reliably at a sustainable cost.

The CPAP, Bluetooth, Apple Health, and Sleep Cycle ideas are candidate ways to deliver value. The business plan must connect each proposed feature to a customer outcome, evidence of demand, feasible access to data, and a viable way to reach customers.

**This document plans future work. It contains no completed market research, customer validation, market-size estimate, revenue forecast, or legal assessment.** See [[2026-11-11 - Business planning scope]].

## 2. Starting position

Confirmed context: the founder uses AirSense 11 and myAir, has relevant technical experience, wants to explore a business, and previously prioritized direct Bluetooth. The repository and Obsidian vault exist.

Unresolved: the precise personal problem, first buyer, launch geography, business ambition, available time/cash, willingness to pay, supported data access, and commercial obligations. No product or integrations exist.

These are decisions to resolve through the programme. Sweden is a possible starting market based on the founder's context; it has not been selected. B2C and B2B are separate hypotheses with different buying processes. Do not combine their demand estimates or apply the same sales timetable to both.

## 3. How to manage the programme

- **Founder:** accountable for direction, access to personal experience, time and budget limits, customer relationships, and investment decisions.
- **Development/research support:** prepare briefs, research when commissioned, organize evidence, model options, and implement the approved scope.
- **Specialist input:** arrange clinical, privacy, regulatory, legal, accounting, or security review when the chosen use case requires it. No adviser has been appointed.
- **Weekly review:** what did we learn, what contradicts the idea, which assumption matters most next, and what decision follows?
- **Gate review:** record Continue, Narrow, Rework, Hold, or Stop with reasons in [[Decision log]]. A Hold or Stop applies to the candidate investment, not a claim that all other possibilities are exhausted.
- **Budget release:** authorize a separate cash ceiling and founder-hour ceiling for each stage. No spend, customer outreach, payment collection, or public launch is authorized merely by this plan.

The numbers and timing below are proposed management targets. Agree them before execution. Small samples guide the next investment decision; they do not establish population demand or product-market fit.

## 4. Roadmap and dependencies

The sample cadence assumes part-time founder involvement of roughly 8–10 hours per week and access to participants. Capacity is unconfirmed. Calendar periods move if those assumptions fail; evidence gates remain in place.

| Phase | Indicative cadence | Principal output | Investment decision |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0. Founder brief | Week 1 | Specific problem, ambition, constraints | Which opportunity should we investigate? |
| 1. Market and customer research | Weeks 2–4 | Evidence pack and initial segment choice | Is there a sufficiently important and reachable unmet need? |
| 2. Data and delivery feasibility | Weeks 3–5, alongside phase 1 | Feasible route and delivery-risk assessment | Can we deliver the outcome legitimately and reliably? |
| 3. Offer and business economics | Weeks 5–6 | Positioning, offer, price experiment, economic model | Is a commercial test justified? |
| 4. Outcome prototype | Weeks 6–8 | Usability and value evidence | Does the proposed experience solve the selected problem? |
| 5. Paid-pilot readiness | Weeks 8–9, only after gates | Narrow product scope and operating readiness | Are we ready to accept paying participants? |
| 6. Controlled paid pilot | Weeks 10–13 if ready | Payment, repeat-use, support, and delivery evidence | Continue, change the offer, or stop? |
| 7. Limited launch | Months 4–6, conditional | Repeatable acquisition and service delivery | Can growth be funded and supported? |
| 8. Expansion decision | Month 6 onward, conditional | Evidence-based growth or consolidation plan | Which investment has the strongest case? |

Phases 1 and 2 can overlap. A low-fidelity concept can be tested before a full connector exists. Real participant health-data collection, payment, and external launch each require their own readiness decision. A clinic-led route may take substantially longer; build its timetable after learning the buying process.

## 5. Phase 0 — define the founder's opportunity

### Activities

Write one real episode in [[Founder problem]]: trigger, desired outcome, obstacle, current workaround, frequency, and impact. Identify what the founder has already tried. Separate the underlying need from a preferred technology.

Choose the intended ambition, weekly capacity, affordable discovery budget, earliest review date, and acceptable downside. Clarify the equipment, accounts, code, and other resources available for independent commercial use, including any relevant employment or ownership constraints.

Create two or three candidate customer/problem statements. Identify the user, payer, and purchasing decision-maker for each. Select a geography to investigate and explain why it is accessible.

### Deliverables and gate

A one-page founder brief, ranked assumptions, and stage-1 budget/timebox. Continue when a concrete experience and an identifiable audience are clear enough to investigate. If the problem remains broad, refine the brief before designing the product.

## 6. Phase 1 — commission market and customer research

### Activities

Execute [[Market research brief]] only when this phase is started. Combine primary interviews, an assessment of current alternatives, reachable-market sizing, and investigation of distribution.

Proposed starting sample: 15–20 individual CPAP users across relevant experience and technology-use groups. Review after the first five interviews to improve the questions. Narrow the sample once a promising segment emerges. Add 3–5 ecosystem interviews with clinicians, distributors, or other relevant participants if those perspectives are needed; do not treat them as evidence that organizations will buy.

Investigate what people currently do, the cost of that behavior, why alternatives are accepted or rejected, and the circumstances under which they would switch. Include people who do not want another app and people satisfied with current tools.

Compare existing solutions on the same user task. Assess price, workflow, data requirements, onboarding effort, trust, and geographic applicability. Build market estimates from the number of relevant, reachable customers and explicit assumptions rather than treating everyone with a condition as a potential subscriber.

### Deliverables

- Research dossier with dated sources, interview method, limitations, and contrary evidence.
- Segment comparison, competitor/alternative matrix, and bottom-up opportunity model.
- Evidence-backed recommendation for one initial customer segment and outcome.

### Gate

Before interviewing, set the evidence threshold with the founder. A proposed signal for further testing is at least five independent people in the same segment describing a similar recent problem, including at least three with an observable workaround or meaningful cost. Record the denominator and counterexamples. This is a discovery signal, not a market-size estimate or sales forecast.

Continue when the problem recurs, existing alternatives leave a meaningful gap, and there is a plausible way to reach the chosen segment. Rework the segment/problem if evidence is mixed. Stop investment in that hypothesis if the problem has low impact or existing options satisfy the audience.

## 7. Phase 2 — prove a deliverable and assess trust requirements

### Activities

Map the minimum data needed to answer the selected question. Investigate access, permission to use the data, format, reliability, latency, onboarding effort, ongoing maintenance, and third-party costs.

Use [[Data source feasibility]] to compare direct BLE, SD-card import, authorized exports or interfaces, Apple Health, and Sleep Cycle. Direct Bluetooth remains a preference to investigate. Agree an initial feasibility ceiling, for example eight founder-hours, before beginning the BLE spike. Discovery or pairing alone is not sufficient evidence of usable therapy records.

Test a confirmed route with the founder's own permitted data before taking participant records. Report the actual result of each device step. If BLE cannot provide an appropriate route within the ceiling, record why and assess a fallback against the same customer outcome.

Write the intended product purpose and the claims it would make. Commission assessment of applicable privacy, medical-device, consumer, data-access, licensing, and other commercial requirements. Translate the result into delivery scope, controls, costs, and prohibited claims. Do not infer clearance from calling a product “wellness.”

### Deliverables and gate

A documented route for one real session, a reproducible import, a realistic customer onboarding path, preliminary delivery cost, and a specialist-assessment action list with owners.

Continue when the selected outcome is feasible through an acceptable route and no unresolved issue makes the proposed next test inappropriate. Limit this conclusion to the tested setup. If no viable route exists, revise the use case or stop that solution direction before product development.

## 8. Phase 3 — choose positioning, offer, and economic model

### Activities

State the offer in plain language: for whom, in which situation, what result, why the current alternative is inadequate, what is included, and what it costs. Choose one initial business model from [[Business model hypotheses]].

Prepare two or three positioning/offer options for qualitative testing. Design pricing tests around a clear deliverable and a real purchasing choice, using customer spending and alternatives as inputs. Distinguish interest, waitlist sign-up, pilot commitment, payment, and renewal.

Build [[Financial model plan]] with downside, base, and upside assumptions. Include support and onboarding labor, integration maintenance, acquisition costs, payment costs, cash timing, and founder compensation. Set a maximum affordable pilot loss.

### Deliverables and gate

A one-page commercial offer, positioning statement, price-test protocol, channel hypothesis, and scenario model.

Continue when there is a specific buyer and outcome, a testable offer, some behavior supporting a further commercial test, and a credible path to positive contribution. A free waitlist or interview enthusiasm alone does not satisfy this gate. If the offer has no response, allow one bounded revision before further investment.

## 9. Phase 4 — test the customer outcome with a small prototype

### Activities

Choose a prototype that resolves the remaining uncertainty: a workflow mockup, a clearly described manual service, or a thin functional application. Use synthetic examples for concept tests and label them. Use real records only within the data-handling arrangements agreed for that test.

Proposed sample: 5–8 people from the selected segment. Give each a concrete task based on their actual question. Observe completion, interpretation, time to a useful result, onboarding friction, trust concerns, and comparison with their current method.

Test what the product helps people do; logins and attractive charts are insufficient measures of value. AI is optional and needs evidence that it improves the selected outcome. A second data source is added when it is necessary to that outcome.

### Deliverables and gate

A usability/value report, revised offer, and prioritized scope in [[MVP options]]. Define success before sessions. A possible prototype gate is four of five participants completing the key task without founder explanation and articulating why the result is useful. Also review errors and misunderstanding; task completion alone is insufficient.

Continue when the outcome and workflow justify a small commercial pilot. Rework once where the failure is specific and remediable. Stop a solution that repeatedly produces no value or unreliable interpretations.

## 10. Phase 5 — make a paid pilot operationally ready

### Activities

Approve a small statement of work: one segment, one core job, a confirmed data route, supported devices/setups, support boundaries, exclusions, and measurable acceptance criteria. Estimate delivery effort before building.

Develop only the scope required for that pilot. Prepare participant onboarding, a support process, data access/deletion/export, backups and recovery, incident handling, and clear explanations of limitations. Test these arrangements before relying on them.

Set up the commercial arrangements appropriate to the chosen route: seller/business structure, ownership of work, accounting and tax handling, payment provider, terms, cancellation/refund arrangements, appropriate insurance assessment, and any specialist actions identified in phase 2. Confirm the working name and intended brand can be used before investing in launch assets.

### Deliverables and gate

A pilot-ready product/service, operating checklist, participant information, support owner, cash ceiling, and signed-off unresolved-issue list.

Accept payment only after the founder has confirmed readiness and the relevant specialist actions are resolved for that scope. There should be no unresolved critical issue affecting delivery, participant data, claims, or payment handling.

## 11. Phase 6 — run a controlled paid pilot

### Activities

For a B2C route, a proposed starting cohort is 5–10 paying participants over four weeks, with the task frequency defined in advance. For B2B, design a separate pilot with a small number of organizations and an identified buyer; do not assume the B2C timetable applies.

Deliver the agreed outcome, record support minutes and costs, monitor failures and dropouts, and ask participants why they return or stop. Evaluate continuation at the stated price, including the effect of discounts and founder attention.

### Deliverables and gate

A pilot review covering acquisition source, consented participation, activation, outcome achievement, repeat use, actual payment, continuation, support load, incidents, and contribution.

Example B2C learning target to agree before recruitment: three of the first five paying participants still use the core outcome in week four, where weekly use is appropriate, and three choose paid continuation at the disclosed price. Report the denominator and selection bias. Four weeks does not establish long-term retention, lifetime value, or product-market fit.

Proceed to limited launch only with a convincing combination of customer value, payment/continuation behavior, affordable support, reliable delivery, and a feasible acquisition hypothesis. Otherwise narrow, revise, or end the pilot.

## 12. Phase 7 — launch in one segment through one primary channel

### Activities

Turn the successful pilot into a repeatable onboarding and support process. Choose one primary acquisition channel and a small controlled budget; test its message and conversion path before adding channels. Candidate channels are research questions: relevant communities with permission, useful educational content, referrals, partnerships, or targeted advertising.

Build a trustworthy public explanation, demo, offer, and customer support path. Publish only after the founder approves the concrete release and operating arrangements. Measure the full funnel from source to useful outcome and payment.

Review cohorts over a longer period, including cancellation reasons, contribution after support, and acquisition spending. Keep cash forecasts current and monitor capacity.

### Gate

Increase spending only when the customer outcome repeats, service quality is stable, and channel/cohort evidence supports the economics. Establish thresholds from actual pilot costs rather than adopting an arbitrary growth target. Address churn or support bottlenecks before buying more traffic.

## 13. Phase 8 — choose expansion, consolidation, or exit

Evaluate new data sources, device coverage, markets, distribution partners, clinic workflows, or licensing only against demonstrated demand and maintenance capacity. Each new customer segment needs its own buying-process evidence. Each material change in intended purpose or product claims needs review.

Assess defensibility through trusted workflows, useful expertise, reliable integrations, and customer relationships. Do not assume a multi-source dashboard, an AI feature, or health-data accumulation creates a durable advantage.

Compare bootstrapping from revenue with other funding options only after documenting the use of funds, milestones it would buy, cash downside, and founder objectives. Maintain a credible option to remain a small profitable service or discontinue an uneconomic product.

Deliver an updated strategy, operating budget, and next investment decision. Scale is an option earned by evidence.

## 14. Ongoing management after launch

Use a lightweight recurring cadence: weekly customer/support and cash review; monthly acquisition/cohort economics, competitor/price changes, and vendor/interface dependency review; quarterly strategy, market assumptions, positioning, capacity, and funding review. Adjust frequency to the size of the business.

Repeat targeted customer research when retention, acquisition, support, or switching behavior changes. Reassess scope and the relevant commercial requirements when entering a new geography, serving a new buyer type, adding a data source, or changing intended purpose or claims. Keep a current source date and owner for each material assumption.

## 15. First planning actions

1. Complete [[Founder problem]] with one episode.
2. Set ambition, first geography, weekly hours, and separate cash/hour ceilings for discovery.
3. Rank the assumptions in [[Risk and assumption register]].
4. Approve the scope and evidence criteria in [[Market research brief]].
5. Create the research tasks in [[Execution backlog]] and assign a weekly review date.
6. Start research only when commissioned; review the first evidence before funding a full product.
